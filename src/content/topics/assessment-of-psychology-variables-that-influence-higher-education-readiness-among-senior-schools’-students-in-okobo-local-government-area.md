  ABSTRACT 

  This study investigated the psychological variables influencing higher education readiness among senior secondary school students in Okobo Local Government Area, Akwa Ibom State

  . The transition to tertiary education is a multidimensional process requiring not only academic preparedness but also social and emotional competencies

  . The primary objective of the research was to assess the influence of four key variables: collaboration, self-esteem, emotional intelligence, and emotional resilience

  .

  The study adopted a descriptive survey research design

  . The target population comprised approximately 3,200 Senior Secondary Two (SS2) students across 15 public schools

  . A sample of 200 students was selected from four schools using a stratified random sampling technique to ensure representation across science, arts, and commercial academic streams

  . Data were collected using two validated instruments: the Psychological Variables Questionnaire (PVQ) and the Higher Education Readiness Test (HERT), which yielded reliability coefficients of 0.82 and 0.79, respectively

  .

  The theoretical framework for the study was grounded in Albert Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory, emphasizing self-efficacy, and Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs, focusing on the fulfillment of esteem and belongingness

  . Data analysis involved the use of mean, standard deviation, and t-tests to evaluate the research hypotheses

  . The study aims to address the gap in research regarding rural educational settings, where students often face unique socio-economic and psychological barriers during their transition to university life

  . Findings from this study are intended to assist educators, counselors, and policymakers in developing tailored interventions to enhance student preparedness

INTRODUCTION

1.1      Background of the Study

Education is a critical tool forboth individual and societal development, serving as a pathway for individualsto achieve personal aspirations while also contributing meaningfully to theircommunities. This essential process begins with the transition from high schoolto higher education, which is a significant milestone in students' lives. Thetransition from high school to higher education is a pivotal moment that canset the course for an individual's future. According to Chen (2016) in *TheCondition of Education Report*, students who successfully navigate this transitionoften possess a strong sense of purpose regarding their educational goals. Thisclarity in aspiration empowers them to pursue higher education, where they gainvaluable skills and knowledge that enhance their employability and socialmobility. Higher education is not merely an individual benefit; it has profoundimplications for societal growth, as educated individuals are more likely toparticipate in community development and contribute to economic vitality.

The economic impact of higher educationat a societal level cannot be overstated. The Organisation for EconomicCo-operation and Development (OECD) in its report *Education at a Glance2018* notes that higher education benefits both individuals andsociety. Each additional year of education correlates with improved economicoutput due to a more skilled workforce. As individuals achieve higher educationlevels, they are better equipped to secure well-paying jobs, contribute toeconomic growth, and reduce reliance on social services. In this way, educationserves as a catalyst for economic development and poverty alleviation, creatinga more prosperous society.

Furthermore, education plays acritical role in fostering civic engagement and social responsibility. Densonand Chang (2019) posit that students who are engaged in higher education aremore likely to participate in civic activities and exhibit a sense of socialresponsibility. This engagement fosters a culture of activism andcollaboration, with educated individuals more likely to contribute to theircommunities as informed and engaged citizens. Higher education institutions canenhance these opportunities by integrating community service and civic learninginto their curricula, fostering a commitment to social change among students. 

Readinessfor higher education is a multidimensional construct that encompassescognitive, social, and psychological factors. For senior secondary schoolstudents, the transition to higher education requires not only academicpreparation but also emotional resilience, interpersonal skills, and culturaladaptability. According to Conley (2022), academic readiness is a criticalcomponent of this transition, but emotional and social readiness also play asignificant role in students' ability to succeed in a higher educationenvironment. The psychological factors that influence this transition as usedin this study include self-esteem, collaboration, emotional intelligence andemotional resilience. 

Collaboration,including communication, teamwork, and interpersonal engagement, is crucial forstudents transitioning to higher education, as it significantly impacts bothacademic and social integration. Effective communication within collaborativesettings allows students to articulate their thoughts clearly, participate indiscussions, and engage with professors and peers. According to Scherer et al.(2021), students who excel in collaborative environments tend to perform betteracademically due to their ability to work effectively in group settings, which isa key component of many higher education curricula. These students are alsomore likely to seek out and form study groups, fostering cooperative learningthat can deepen their understanding of course material. Moreover, teamwork andinterpersonal engagement are vital for building relationships with faculty,leading to mentorship opportunities, academic guidance, and a stronger sense ofbelonging within the academic community (Zhao & Kuh, 2022). As universitiesincreasingly emphasize interdisciplinary work, students with strongcollaborative abilities are better able to navigate diverse learningenvironments, contribute to team-based projects, and engage in academicpartnerships across disciplines (Terenzini et al., 2021). In addition, studentswho actively engage in collaboration are more likely to participate inextracurricular activities, enhancing their overall experience and sense ofbelonging within the institution, which in turn can contribute to higherretention and graduation rates (Baker et al., 2022). Thus, fosteringcollaboration plays a crucial role in students' academic success and overallcollege experience.

Self-esteemremains another central factor influencing students' academic readiness, as itdirectly impacts their academic motivation, engagement, and overall success.High self-esteem fosters a positive outlook on challenges, encouraging studentsto embrace difficult tasks and persist despite setbacks. When students feelconfident in their abilities, they are more likely to seek out new opportunitiesfor growth, engage in problem-solving, and develop a sense of ownership overtheir learning. Hill and Wang (2015) suggest that students with positiveself-esteem exhibit stronger resilience, as they are less likely to internalizefailures or setbacks as permanent deficiencies. This positive mindset leads togreater academic success and personal growth. Conversely, students with lowself-esteem may lack the belief in their own potential, leading to avoidance ofdifficult tasks, self-doubt, and a lack of participation in classroomdiscussions or extracurricular activities. This can hinder their readiness forhigher education, where the expectations for independent learning andself-motivation are much higher. Low self-esteem often correlates with greateracademic stress and anxiety, which can diminish students’ ability to focus onacademic tasks and negatively impact their performance (Schunk, 2022).

Emotional intelligence plays a fundamental role indetermining how well students navigate the academic, social, and personalchallenges associated with higher education. Defined as the ability torecognize, understand, and regulate emotions while also being able to perceiveand influence the emotions of others (Mayer, Caruso, & Salovey, 2016), emotionalintelligence contributes to students' capacity to manage stress, buildrelationships, and engage effectively in academic environments. Students withhigh emotional intelligence are better prepared to cope with academic pressuresand social transitions, which are common in higher education (Schutte &Malouff, 2017). Conversely, students with lower emotional intelligence maystruggle with emotional regulation, making them more vulnerable to stress,anxiety, and disengagement from academic activities (Zeidner, Matthews, &Roberts, 2019).

            A key aspect of emotionalintelligence is self-regulation, which helps students manage emotions underpressure. Many students entering higher education experience stress due tocompetitive academic environments, coursework demands, and new socialexpectations (MacCann et al., 2020). Those with strong self-regulation skillsare better equipped to handle these stressors, reducing the likelihood ofacademic burnout and emotional exhaustion (Mikolajczak, Gross, & Roskam,2019). For example, a student who recognizes early signs of academic stress andemploys coping mechanisms such as structured time management, mindfulness, oracademic support-seeking behaviors is more likely to maintain motivation andpersistence (Pekrun & Loderer, 2020). In contrast, students with poorself-regulation may struggle with procrastination, avoidance behaviors, orself-doubt, negatively impacting their academic success and readiness forhigher education (Trezise & Reeve, 2018).

            Additionally, emotional intelligenceis crucial for effective interpersonal relationships in academic settings.Students who can communicate well, display empathy, and navigate socialinteractions tend to perform better in collaborative learning environments,engage more actively in classroom discussions, and seek academic assistancewhen needed (Kirk, Schutte, & Hine, 2018). This is particularly relevant indiverse learning environments, where students must interact with peers fromdifferent cultural and socio-economic backgrounds (Malik & Shujja, 2021).Those with higher emotional intelligence can navigate these differences withgreater adaptability, fostering inclusion and mutual understanding, whereasstudents with lower emotional intelligence may struggle with social integrationand feelings of isolation (Fiori & Vesely-Maillefer, 2018).

Closely linked to emotional intelligence is resilience, whichrefers to an individual's ability to recover from setbacks, adapt to change,and persist despite challenges (Fletcher & Sarkar, 2016). Resilience is acrucial psychological factor that enables students to cope with academicfailures, transition to new learning environments, and maintain motivationduring periods of stress (Martin & Marsh, 2019). Research indicates thatstudents with strong resilience are more likely to embrace academic challengesas opportunities for growth rather than perceiving them as barriers (Yeager& Dweck, 2014). This mindset is particularly critical in higher education,where students must manage rigorous academic expectations, financialconstraints, and personal hardships (Masten, 2018).

            For instance, first-generationcollege students or those from disadvantaged socio-economic backgrounds oftenface additional challenges in transitioning to higher education, includingfinancial instability and a lack of academic support (Steinhardt & Dolbier,2020). However, resilient students are more likely to seek alternativesolutions, such as applying for scholarships, engaging in campus mentorshipprograms, and utilizing academic resources to navigate these barriers (Ungar,2021). In contrast, students with low resilience may feel overwhelmed by thesechallenges, increasing their likelihood of academic disengagement or droppingout (Cahill et al., 2020). Developing resilience through structured supportsystems, mentorship, and exposure to growth-oriented learning strategies hasbeen shown to significantly enhance students' ability to persist in highereducation (Richardson, Suinn, & Bouchey, 2019).

Despitethe acknowledgment of psychological variables, many educational systems focuspredominantly on cognitive assessments, overlooking the holistic nature ofreadiness. The disparity between academic and psychological preparedness canresult in high dropout rates or underperformance among students who lack therequisite social and emotional competencies. In Okobo Local Government Area,socio-economic disparities and cultural dynamics pose unique challenges toeducational transitions. This study aims to investigate psychological variablesthat influence higher education readiness among senior schools’ students inOkobo Local Government Area

 

1.2       Statement of the Problem

The transition from senior secondaryschool to higher education is a crucial phase in a student's academic journey,yet many students in Okobo Local Government Area face significant challengesthat hinder their ability to successfully navigate this transition. Thesechallenges are not merely academic in nature but are deeply intertwined withcritical psychological factors such as collaboration skills, self-esteem,emotional intelligence, and emotional resilience.

            One of themajor issues is the lack of effective collaboration skills among students.Collaboration is essential in higher education, where group projects, peerinteractions, and cooperative learning environments are commonplace. However,many students in Okobo struggle with working effectively with others, whichimpedes their ability to engage in productive group activities, build supportiverelationships, and contribute meaningfully to academic discussions. This lackof collaboration skills limits their ability to adapt to the social andacademic demands of higher education.

            Additionally,many students in Okobo suffer from low self-esteem, which further complicatestheir transition to higher education. Students with low self-esteem tend tohave negative perceptions of their abilities, which erodes their motivation andacademic aspirations. These students often fear failure and avoid challenges,resulting in disengagement from academic activities and a diminished sense ofconfidence in their potential for success in higher education. This lack ofself-belief severely hinders their readiness for the academic rigors and socialcomplexities of university life.

            Emotionalintelligence, the ability to understand, regulate, and manage one’s emotions,is another psychological variable that affects students’ preparedness forhigher education. Many students in Okobo lack the emotional intelligencenecessary to cope with the pressures of university life, including academicstress, social integration, and personal growth. Without emotionalintelligence, students are more vulnerable to stress, anxiety, and socialisolation, which further undermines their ability to thrive in higher educationsettings.

            Moreover,emotional resilience, or the ability to bounce back from setbacks and perseverein the face of adversity, is a critical skill for navigating the challenges ofhigher education. Students in Okobo who lack resilience are less likely to copeeffectively with academic difficulties, personal challenges, or the pressuresof independent living that often accompany university life. This lack ofresilience leads to higher dropout rates, academic burnout, and disengagementfrom the educational process.

            Together,these psychological barriers create significant obstacles for students in OkoboLocal Government Area, preventing them from making a successful transition tohigher education. These challenges not only hinder their academic performancebut also limit their ability to engage socially, form relationships, and managethe personal adjustments required in higher education.

            Given theimportance of psychological preparedness in ensuring success in highereducation, it is critical to address these issues to improve the transitionprocess for students in Okobo. Without addressing these psychological barriers,many students will continue to face difficulties in adjusting to highereducation, leading to reduced opportunities for personal, academic, andprofessional growth. Therefore, this study seeks to investigate the influenceof these psychological variables on higher education readiness among seniorsecondary school students in Okobo Local Government Area and propose strategiesto better equip them for this critical transition.

While the study examines thesepsychological variables, it does not explore cognitive or economic aspects ofreadiness in detail. The research is limited to these specific psychologicalfactors within Okobo LGA.
